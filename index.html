Eligibility

Who qualifies for SSDI vs. SSI?

When eligibility is based on disability, both programs use SSA's adult disability standard. SSI can also qualify someone based on blindness or age 65 or older without requiring a disability. The programs otherwise differ in work history, income, resources and what happens after approval.

SSDI

Who qualifies for SSDI?

SSDI looks at disability and your insured work history. Unlike SSI, savings, investments and household assets do not set SSDI eligibility.

  1. 01

    Your medical condition prevents substantial gainful activity and prevents you from doing past work or adjusting to other work. It must have lasted, or be expected to last, at least 12 months or result in death.

  2. 02

    You have enough Social Security work credits for your age and enough recent covered work. The common benchmark is 40 credits with 20 earned in the last 10 years, but younger workers may qualify with fewer.

  3. 03

    You are under full retirement age. If you are receiving SSDI when you reach that age, SSA converts the benefit to retirement benefits.

Key distinction: SSDI is earned through covered work. The monthly amount follows your earnings record, rather than a financial-need calculation.

SSI

Who qualifies for SSI?

SSI focuses on age or disability together with limited income and resources. Work history is not part of the test.

  1. 01

    You are age 65 or older, blind, or have a qualifying disability. People age 65 or older do not need to establish disability.

  2. 02

    Your countable resources stay within SSI limits: $2,000 for an individual or $3,000 for a couple. Your home, household goods and one vehicle used for household transportation generally do not count.

  3. 03

    Your countable income stays within SSI rules. Earned income, unearned income and in-kind support are counted differently, with general and earned-income exclusions applied before the final calculation.

Other SSI requirements

You must be a U.S. citizen or national, or meet one of SSA's qualifying noncitizen classifications. You must also meet SSA's U.S. residency and absence rules.

The exact income calculation depends on the type of income you receive and your living arrangement. Record assets and support accurately so SSA can apply the correct exclusions and reductions.

Monthly benefits

How Much Does Each Program Pay?

Payment depends on the program and your circumstances. Each amount stays connected to its label, year and conditions.

SSDI

How SSDI is calculated

SSA uses your average indexed monthly earnings to calculate a Primary Insurance Amount. Your benefit can be higher or lower than the national average because the calculation is individual to your work record.

  • Savings, investments and most other income do not reduce an SSDI payment.
  • Work earnings can affect disability eligibility and continued benefits under separate work-incentive rules.
  • Workers' compensation and certain public disability benefits can reduce SSDI when combined benefits exceed the applicable limit.
SSI

How SSI is adjusted

SSI starts with the federal maximum and reduces from there based on countable income. The actual payment can be lower because of earned or unearned income, a spouse's or parent's income, and some living arrangements.

  • Some states add a supplement to the federal payment.
  • North Carolina Special Assistance supports some eligible low-income adults in approved residential facilities.
  • North Carolina also has an In-Home option for some adults who are at risk of entering a residential facility. A county Department of Social Services determines eligibility and payment.

2026 reference: Review SSA's COLA fact sheet, SSI payment guidance, and North Carolina's Special Assistance programs.

Healthcare

Medicare vs. Medicaid.

SSDI recipients generally qualify for Medicare after the applicable waiting period. SSI recipients qualify for Medicaid in most states, often automatically after approval.

Coverage generally begins after the applicable waiting period. Medicare includes hospital, outpatient and physician services, and prescription drug coverage, with premiums and cost-sharing.

Different timing rules apply for ALS and end-stage renal disease.

In most states, SSI eligibility connects to Medicaid, often automatically. Coverage and costs vary by state and may include medical, hospital, prescription and long-term care services.
